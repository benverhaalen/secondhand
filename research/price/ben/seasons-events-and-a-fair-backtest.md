# How should we backtest prices so the result flatters us and stays true, and how do seasons, waves and dated events fit in?
Researched 2026-11-15 by Ben (Claude Code agent, pitch thread "Frame the problems and beat competitors"). Feature: price.

Ben, 07:18 UTC: "considering a backtest, how would you set this up in a flattering but truthful way. like average price changes over season ... possible spikes in either buying or sales on the resale market. i feel like it goes in waves ... some could be dates too. see if theres any rdd for this" ([words](../../../product/words.md#2026-11-15-a-backtest-that-flatters-truthfully-seasons-and-waves-and-merge-to-main)). Builds on [risk-and-reconciliation.md](risk-and-reconciliation.md) (the accuracy report) and the existing cards [money D3](../../../spec/determinations/money.md#d3-time-adjustment), [D6](../../../spec/determinations/money.md#d6-range-calibration-and-display-state), [M12](../../../spec/determinations/market-insight.md#m12-seasonality) and [M16](../../../spec/determinations/market-insight.md#m16-how-new-retail-releases-relate-to-used-supply-and-price), and does not repeat them.

Method: RDD with IAE. Marks: *observed* (opened, says so), *asserted* (a source says it without showing, or from memory), *inferred* (my reasoning).

**Draft before searching:** "pick a good test window, compare against a weak baseline, show the season as a nice curve." **What changed:** a hand-picked window or a weak baseline is exactly what makes a test untrue, and the honest moves are themselves the flattering ones. Zillow's own split shows it: the error on homes it can see listed is a quarter of the error on homes it can't, and it publishes both. Forecasters already model "waves" as a yearly curve plus a named list of dated events with windows, each with its own size, which is the form we can explain to a person in one sentence.

## How I would set it up (agent proposal)

Flattering and truthful are not in tension if the flattery comes from choosing the comparison a seller actually faces and the honesty comes from showing everything.

1. **Freeze the test before running it.** The metric, the cells, the baselines and the pass bar are written down and committed first (D6 already fixes the bar). Nothing is chosen after seeing the result. This is what makes any later number believable.
2. **Compare against what a seller does today.** Three baselines on the same sales: the last sale of the same product, the eBay-style 90-day sold median (both already in D6), and seasonal naive (the same product's median in the same month last year). Report skill as `1 - our error / baseline error`. "Our price misses by 40% less than the eBay sold median" is flattering because that median is the real alternative, and true because it is.
3. **Lead with the well-informed case, show the cold case next to it.** Zillow publishes 1.80% for listed homes and 7.21% for unlisted ones on one page (*observed*); part of that gap is anchoring, because the list price feeds the estimate (*inferred*). We report sales of items listed with us, leaving out sales at exactly our suggested ask, and sales elsewhere of items priced from their photos and title, side by side. The first will look better and is honest, because it is the case the product is built for and the anchoring is removed or stated.
4. **Count the times we refused.** Hit rate sits next to accuracy (D6 step 4). "We price 82% of items and hold back on the rest" is a strength when stated.
5. **Every cell, every month, with its n.** Lead the page with the strongest categories, but publish the full table with counts and the misses list. No window is picked; the test period is every month since the first frozen estimate.
6. **Show predictions we made in advance.** The most persuasive honest artefact is a forecast frozen and published before the outcome: "In October we said coats would sell about 15% above the year's average in November. They did 12%." This turns the backtest into a running record (prospective, like D6 step 1).
7. **Show the calendar's own value.** Run the backtest twice, with and without the season and event terms. "Knowing the calendar cuts our error by X%" is the claim that tests Ben's waves idea, and it might come back near zero for some categories; then we say so.

## Waves and dated events (agent proposal)

- **Form:** the category price and sales index (M14) = trend + yearly season (M12) + a named list of dated events, each with a window and its own effect, fitted Prophet-style. Each event is shown as a sentence: "Jewelry sells about 18% faster in the two weeks before Valentine's Day."
- **Starting event list, each kept only if our data shows an effect larger than its noise:** tax refund weeks (mid-February to April), Black Friday to Cyber Monday, the Christmas shipping cut-off, January returns and resolutions (fitness gear), Valentine's Day, back to school (August), Prime Day, the February and September fashion weeks, Thanksgiving, and per product its release and restock dates (M2, M16). A first cold week for coats is an event from weather, not the calendar (*inferred*).
- **Humans are predictable in dates:** paydays, refunds and holidays come at known times, and forecasters give them their own terms (*observed* in Prophet and M5); that this is why is my reading (*inferred*). The sales side may wave as well as the price side: refunds raise buying, January clear-outs raise listing. Both are measured: sold counts (demand), new listings (supply) and price.
- **Telling the person:** a wave chart, with the line of real prices, the shaded usual season, and pins on the dated events, plus one sentence on what the next pin means for them now: "Tax refunds start landing in two weeks; electronics usually sell faster then. Listing now gets you in early."
- **Guard:** when the market breaks (a fee change, a platform rule, a viral moment), the season and event estimates are flagged and the backtest says so; Prophet's authors note error estimates are "essentially useless" across an abrupt change (*observed*).
