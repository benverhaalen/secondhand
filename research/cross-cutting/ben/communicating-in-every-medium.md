# How should the words change from one medium to the next, and what holds in all of them?
Researched 2026-11-15 by Ben (Claude Code, thread "Build the brand system for everything"), by three smaller agents, one question each. Feature: cross-cutting (brand).

Ben ([words](../../../product/words.md#2026-11-15-writing-changes-by-medium-the-right-fonts-showing-not-telling-and-general-rules-first)): "writing should change based on medium", "research should back", "showing not telling", "creating generalizable rules that fit all this".

Marks: [O] observed (the agent opened the source and read it there), [A] asserted (memory, or a second-hand summary), [I] inferred.

**Decision it feeds:** "What each medium is for" and "What holds in every medium" in [Part III](../../../brand/constitution.md#part-iii-media), and judge lines G7 to G10.

## Answer in short

1. The voice stays and the tone and length change. What sets the length is what the reader is doing and how long they give it [O, Apple, Mailchimp].
2. The best-supported rules are about showing: words with a relevant picture beat words alone, extras hurt, a label belongs next to its thing, and one signal per view helps [O, Mayer]. These come from lessons, not pitches [I].
3. On a slide, a sentence headline over visual evidence beat a topic label over bullets in three studies [O, Alley]. No source sets a word count; every number for slides is a proposal.
4. Several popular numbers are not usable: the learning pyramid, the 10 minute attention span, the 7 percent rule [O, A].

## Where the sources disagree

A sentence headline (Alley) against almost no words (Kawasaki, the Apple keynote as described). Both fit one sentence of 8 words or fewer over the evidence, which is the limit Article 12.4 already sets. Tufte argues for more on a slide; his case is for a handout a person reads alone [A, not opened].
