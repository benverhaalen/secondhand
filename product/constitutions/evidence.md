# How evidence is judged and shown

The rules every other constitution follows when it gives a reason, uses a number or makes a claim. ## The problem

A rule that says "within 20%" or "70% coverage" tells you a line was drawn, not why it is there or what a person loses if it's crossed. A judge, a teammate or a later agent can't tell whether the line still matters, and a seller can't tell what it means for them. A quote from Ben records who decided, but it doesn't explain the decision. So every reason has to name the cause and what it does to the person, and every number has to earn its place by changing a decision or by being something the person feels.

**Success, checkably:** for any rule in any constitution, a reader can answer "what goes wrong for whom if we break this?" from the rule alone, and every number in it is in units a seller feels or names the decision it moves.

## Rules

**E1. A why is a mechanism and a consequence.** Write it as "because A causes B, which costs or gives the person C". A quote or a link is where the decision came from, not why it holds. Both go in, and the cause comes first. *Why:* a rule without its cause can't be applied to a new case or retired when the cause goes away, so it either gets broken by accident or kept long after it stopped helping.

**E2. A number stays only if it changes a decision or a person feels it.** Dollars, days, taps and items are felt. A test threshold like "within 20%" is a line we hold ourselves to inside the work, so it carries its reason and stays out of headlines. *Why:* a number nobody can act on takes up the attention the useful ones need, and an unexplained one reads as made up ([08:59](../words.md#2026-11-15-every-why-explained-numbers-that-help-and-a-statistic-we-can-defend): "those numbers arent even helpful").

**E3. Lead with the outcome in the person's units.** "Sellers kept $X more per item" beats "7 in 10 within 20%". *Why:* a seller decides by what lands in their account and how long it takes, so a result in those units can be checked against their own experience, and a percentage of a percentage can't.

**E4. Every result is compared with what people do today, on the same items.** The baseline is the thing a person would really do: list at the eBay sold median, post on one site, take their own photos. *Why:* a number alone says nothing about whether the product helps. The comparison is the claim, and running both on the same items keeps luck in the mix of items from passing for skill.

**E5. Defend it before you claim it.** Each headline names how it was measured, the assumption it rests on most heavily, what would make it false, and the cheapest way it could be gamed, with that way ruled out. *Why:* a judge's first question is "how do you know?", and a number that falls to that question costs more trust than having no number at all ([08:59](../words.md#2026-11-15-every-why-explained-numbers-that-help-and-a-statistic-we-can-defend): "creating a statistic and being able to defend it").

**E6. Say where it holds and where it doesn't.** Name the items, sites and conditions a result covers, and the ones it doesn't reach. *Why:* a result read past its scope is a false claim, even when the measurement was honest ([revenue R6](revenue.md)).

**E7. Flattering is allowed; hiding isn't.** We pick the conditions that show our strength, say that we picked them, and publish the result whichever way it goes ([07:18](../words.md#2026-11-15-a-backtest-that-flatters-truthfully-seasons-and-waves-and-merge-to-main)). *Why:* picking where to compete is strategy, but hiding a loss is deception, and once one hidden loss is found every other number gets doubted.

**E8. Every figure carries its mark.** Observed, asserted or inferred, from [features-proof.md](../features-proof.md#how-to-read-the-proof-marks), with a link to where it came from. *Why:* the reader can see in one glance how hard to lean on each number, without having to trust us.
